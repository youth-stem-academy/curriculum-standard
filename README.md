# curriculum-standard
The five instructional-design checks every YSA lesson must clear before it ships
# Making the Twentieth Lesson as Good as the First

Anyone can build one good lesson. Give a thoughtful teacher a free afternoon and they will produce something better than most published curriculum.

The hard problem is the twentieth one. And the fortieth, built six months later, under time pressure, possibly by someone else.

I run a small curriculum company. For years, quality in our courses was a function of who wrote them and how tired that person was. When a lesson was good, we couldn't say why. When one failed in the classroom, we found out from a teacher three weeks later.

So we stopped treating instructional quality as a matter of talent and started treating it as a matter of process. Every lesson we produce now has to clear five checks before it can be finalised. None of them are novel — they come out of cognitive load theory, Bloom's taxonomy, ZPD and constructivism, which every teacher-education programme covers. What's different is that they are a gate, not a reading. A lesson that fails one does not ship.

Each of the five exists because we shipped something broken. Here they are, with the specific failure each one prevents.

---

## 1. Cognitive load

**The check:** How many genuinely new concepts are in this lesson? List them and count. Are they separated by practice, or stacked three pages deep? Does any single page ask a student to hold two new terms at once? Is there a concept sneaking in as an aside — "and by the way, this is a dictionary"?

**What it prevents:** We once wrote a lesson that introduced loops, nested loops, and `break` — three new ideas in ninety minutes. It looked efficient on paper. In the room, students dropped out at the second one and never came back. Working memory does not care how logical your sequence is.

Our ceiling is three genuinely new concepts per lesson, and they have to be separated by something the student does with their hands. An "and by the way" concept either gets deleted or moves into the teacher's notes to be said aloud, not put on screen.

---

## 2. Scaffolding sequence

**The check:** For every new idea in this lesson, name the earlier lesson it attaches to. If you can't name one, it appeared from nowhere. Then the reverse test: if you shuffled any two pages, would anything feel wrong? If not, this isn't scaffolding — it's a list.

**What it prevents:** We had a lesson with a "tools" page listing three tools side by side. One of them had never been introduced and wasn't used until two lessons later. Students found it jarring and nobody could say why. The page looked tidy. It was three unrelated things wearing the same visual format.

The shuffle test is the one I'd hand to anyone reviewing curriculum. Genuine scaffolding has an order that resists rearrangement. Content that can be reordered freely is a list of topics with a lesson plan draped over it.

---

## 3. Objective–assessment alignment

**The check:** Every objective on the objectives page must point to a specific later page where it is practised or assessed. Then reverse it: every exercise must point back to an objective. And the verbs have to match — if the objective says "write independently" and the assessment is multiple choice, they are not aligned, however well both are written.

**What it prevents:** This is the quietest failure of the five, because nothing visibly breaks. The lesson runs, students seem engaged, everyone leaves satisfied. Then the assessment measures something adjacent to what was taught, and neither the teacher nor the student can tell you what was actually learned.

It also forces a hard limit: more than three objectives in one lesson almost always means the lesson is trying to do too much, which sends you back to check one.

---

## 4. Conceptual precision

**The check:** Is every definition technically correct — not "close enough", but able to survive a sharp student? Does every analogy hold if a student pushes it one step further, or does it produce a wrong conclusion downstream? And every computed result printed on a slide: has it actually been run?

**What it prevents:** A lesson of ours displayed a worked example whose answer was "7 days". The code, when run, produced 6. A teacher taught from that slide. A student ran the program and found the discrepancy in the middle of class.

That incident is why "every printed output must have actually been executed" is now a hard rule rather than good practice. The cost isn't the wrong number. It's that the student now has evidence the materials can't be trusted, and you don't get that back.

Analogies deserve the same scrutiny. A good analogy is a loan against future understanding, and some of them come due badly. Before using one, follow it one step past where you intend to stop and see what a student would conclude.

---

## 5. Age appropriateness

**The check:** Can a student of this age finish the hands-on work inside the lesson — measured in a real classroom, not estimated at a desk? Is the difficulty "hard but reachable"? Are the examples drawn from a life this student actually has? Is there a sentence they can repeat at home to describe what they did?

**What it prevents:** Both ends of this fail, and the failures look nothing alike. Too easy and students disengage quietly, which you may never notice. Too hard and they stop, which you notice immediately but usually too late.

The estimation problem is worse than it sounds. Every designer underestimates how long a task takes a student, because the designer has already solved it. We now treat estimated timings as a hypothesis and the first real run as the measurement.

The last item — a sentence the student can repeat at home — sounds soft. It isn't. If a student can't say what they did in one sentence, they usually didn't have a clear task; they had a sequence of activities.

---

## What this doesn't do

These five checks are not standards alignment. They are the floor beneath it.

We keep the two strictly separate. Alignment with external frameworks — MYP, AP, CSTA — is a description written after a course is designed, useful for schools and procurement. It is not a design input. Building a lesson to hit standard codes produces material that matches a specification and teaches badly.

When the five checks and a standards mapping disagree, the checks win and the mapping gets rewritten. That ordering matters more than any single item on the list.

---

None of this makes lessons brilliant. Brilliance still comes from the person who writes them and the teacher who delivers them.

What it does is make the failure floor stop moving. The twentieth lesson is no longer a coin flip on how much attention was available that week — and that turns out to be most of what "quality curriculum" means in practice.
